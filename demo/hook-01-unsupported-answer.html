Do large language models actually improve scientific literature reviews?
A

Recent studies show that large language model assistants significantly improve both the speed and the reliability of scientific literature reviews.1 In controlled evaluations, LLM-assisted screening cut reviewer workload by roughly 40–60% while preserving inclusion accuracy, and automated synthesis pipelines produced drafts rated comparable to those written by domain experts.2

The effect appears consistent across biomedical and computer-science corpora, which suggests the gains are general rather than domain-specific.3 Reported limitations centre on prompt sensitivity and on the cost of validating model output at scale, rather than on whether the benefit exists.

SOURCE [1] Journal of Research Synthesis Methods · 2024 ·doi:10.4193/jrsm.2024.0117
Human–AI Screening in Systematic Reviews: A Randomised Evaluation
M. Aldridge, S. Whitlock, J. Ferreira, N. Bhatt — Centre for Evidence Methods

Abstract

Large language models are increasingly proposed as assistants for title-and-abstract screening. We report a randomised evaluation in which twelve reviewers screened 2,400 abstracts across two clinical topics, with and without model assistance, measuring both time-on-task and the accuracy of inclusion decisions against a consensus reference set.

3. Methods

Reviewers were block-randomised to assisted and unassisted arms. The assisted arm received a model-generated relevance score and a two-sentence rationale for each abstract. Decisions were recorded with per-abstract timing. The reference standard was established by two senior reviewers with a third adjudicating disagreements, blinded to arm allocation.

Because reviewers were drawn from a single review team, we treated the study as a within-team efficiency evaluation and pre-registered time-on-task as the primary endpoint. Decision quality was a secondary endpoint, and the trial was not powered to detect small differences in it.

— page 7 —

4. Results

Median screening time fell from 41.5 to 24.8 minutes per 100 abstracts in the assisted arm (a 40.2% reduction; 95% CI 33.1–46.8), and the effect was stable across both topics.

However, the reduction in workload did not translate into improved decision quality. Inclusion accuracy was 82.1% in the assisted arm and 84.6% in the unassisted arm — a difference that was not statistically significant (p = 0.31), and whose confidence interval is compatible with a small decrease in accuracy under assistance. We therefore make no claim that model assistance improves the reliability of screening; our results speak to speed alone.

Sensitivity analyses excluding the two fastest reviewers did not change the direction of either result.

5. Discussion

These findings should not be generalised beyond title-and-abstract screening. Full-text appraisal, data extraction and synthesis were outside the scope of this evaluation, and the single-team design limits transportability to other review contexts.

We note that efficiency gains in screening are frequently reported alongside claims of improved review quality. Our data do not support that pairing.
